# 2. Literature Review / Related Work

## 2.1 Summary of Key Academic and Industry References

Healthcare digitization has become an important direction in modern medical practice because healthcare depends on accurate, available, and well-organized information. In this context, Electronic Medical Record (EMR) and Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems are designed to replace fragmented paper-based documentation with structured digital records that can store patient demographics, visit notes, diagnoses, prescriptions, laboratory results, imaging reports, attachments, billing information, and follow-up details. Moreover, in large hospitals and advanced healthcare institutions, these systems may also include appointment scheduling, clinical decision support, electronic prescribing, interoperability with laboratories and pharmacies, patient portals, insurance workflows, dashboards, and administrative reporting.

Because medical decisions depend on timely information, the main value of EMR systems is their ability to improve access to patient data at the point of care. A physician can make more informed decisions when previous complaints, diagnoses, medications, investigation results, allergies, and follow-up history are available quickly and clearly. In addition, EMR systems support continuity of care because they preserve the patient's clinical timeline instead of treating each consultation as an isolated event. This is especially important in outpatient specialties such as gynecology, where patients may return for repeated visits, medication review, laboratory interpretation, ultrasound follow-up, treatment monitoring, or pregnancy-related care.

At the broader industry level, the World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes that digital health technologies can contribute to more efficient, sustainable, and equitable health systems when they are designed according to real health system needs and supported by suitable financial, organizational, human, and technological resources (World Health Organization, 2021; World Health Organization, 2025). Therefore, this reference is important for the project because it shows that digital health is not only about using software; it is also about matching the system to its environment. For a small clinic, this means the system should be practical, affordable, focused, and usable without requiring the same infrastructure expected in a large hospital.

However, the literature also shows that adopting EMR and EHR systems is not always simple. Kruse et al. (2016) found that major barriers include initial cost, technical support, technical concerns, resistance to changing work habits, maintenance costs, training needs, privacy concerns, limited time, and workflow challenges. These barriers are especially relevant to small clinics because they often have limited budgets, limited technical support, and a small number of users who must handle clinical, administrative, and documentation tasks at the same time. As a result, a system may be powerful in theory, but if it is expensive, difficult to configure, hard to maintain, or disruptive to daily work, it may fail to be adopted effectively.

Similarly, Rao et al. (2011) studied electronic health records in small physician practices and found that smaller practices were less likely to have fully functional EHR systems and were more likely to report financial barriers and concerns about future obsolescence. This finding further supports the idea that small clinics face different digitalization challenges from large healthcare institutions. In other words, their problem is not only whether digital records are useful, but whether the available systems match their budget, workflow, infrastructure, and long-term capacity.

Beyond cost and infrastructure, workflow fit is another major theme in the literature. Zayas-Caban and Dixon (2010) explain that successful electronic record adoption in small medical practices requires attention to how work is actually organized and performed. Therefore, software should not force a clinic to follow an unsuitable hospital-style process. Instead, it should support the clinic's natural workflow. In a gynecology clinic, this workflow includes patient registration, patient search, profile review, visit documentation, prescription creation, laboratory or ultrasound attachment, billing, and follow-up. For this reason, the patient profile becomes the central workspace because it gathers the information the physician needs during consultation.

In addition to workflow mismatch, documentation burden is a major concern in modern health information systems. Rule et al. (2024) discuss how electronic systems can increase workload when they require excessive typing, repeated data entry, or complex navigation. This point is directly related to the proposed system because digitalization should not simply move paper work onto a screen; rather, it should reduce unnecessary effort. Since outpatient gynecology often includes repeated symptoms, diagnoses, treatment plans, medication combinations, and laboratory requests, reusable case, prescription, and lab templates become important tools for reducing repeated writing while still allowing the physician to edit each case according to the patient's condition.

Alongside EMR systems, clinic management systems are also relevant because they often support daily administrative work such as patient registration, appointments, billing, reporting, and basic medical notes. These systems are usually less complex than hospital EMR platforms and may be more suitable for smaller practices. Nevertheless, many clinic management systems focus more on administration than clinical documentation. They may store patient lists, invoices, or appointments, but they do not always organize symptoms, diagnoses, prescriptions, attachments, and follow-up around the clinical visit. For a gynecology clinic, this is a limitation because the physician needs a clear medical timeline, not only an administrative patient record.

Overall, existing EMR and clinic systems provide useful ideas, but they also show important limitations. Large EMR platforms demonstrate the value of structured medical records, continuity of care, reporting, and integration. Meanwhile, small-practice studies highlight the importance of cost, usability, workflow fit, and practical adoption. Furthermore, documentation-burden research shows that digital systems must reduce repetitive effort, not increase it, while WHO guidance shows that digital health tools should be fit for purpose and realistic for the setting where they will be used. Together, these references support the need for a clinic-focused, workflow-aware digital system that combines patient management, visit documentation, prescriptions, attachments, billing, dashboards, and reusable templates in one practical application.

## 2.2 Identified Gaps and Opportunities for Innovation

The reviewed references show that digital health systems are valuable, but they also reveal clear gaps when viewed from the perspective of a small outpatient gynecology clinic. The proposed Clinic Digitalization System is designed to respond to these gaps directly.

**Gap 1: Existing systems are often too broad or too complex for small clinics.** Large EMR platforms are usually designed for hospitals, multi-provider networks, or advanced institutions. They may include complex modules for insurance, interdepartmental referrals, pharmacy integration, enterprise reporting, and multi-department workflows. These features can be useful in hospitals but unnecessary or difficult to manage in a small private clinic. The opportunity for innovation is to build a focused system that supports the clinic's actual daily workflow without adding hospital-level complexity.

**Gap 2: Many systems are not tailored to gynecology workflows.** General clinic systems may support patient records and appointments, but they often do not reflect the needs of a gynecology clinic. A gynecology workflow may include repeated non-pregnant visits, pregnancy-related visits, treatment monitoring, laboratory review, ultrasound attachments, prescriptions, and follow-up over time. The opportunity is to design the patient profile as the main clinical workspace, where visits, prescriptions, attachments, billing, and follow-up information are connected around the patient's history.

**Gap 3: Paper-based clinics have weak retrieval, continuity, and recovery.** Paper folders can be misplaced, damaged, disorganized, or difficult to search. They also make it hard to recover accidentally removed information or quickly review old visits. The opportunity is to replace physical folders with structured digital records that support patient search, organized visit history, soft delete and restore, and easier access to previous information during consultation.

**Gap 4: Digital systems may still create repetitive documentation work.** Some systems store records digitally but still require the physician to type similar content repeatedly. This limits the practical benefit of digitalization and may increase documentation burden. The opportunity is to use reusable case templates, prescription templates, and lab templates so common symptoms, diagnoses, treatment plans, medication sets, and laboratory requests can be reused, edited, and stored consistently.

**Gap 5: Clinical and administrative records are often separated.** In many paper-based and basic digital workflows, visit notes, prescriptions, attachments, and payments are not clearly connected. A prescription may exist separately from the visit, an ultrasound image may not be linked to the consultation where it was reviewed, and billing may be recorded in a separate notebook or module. The opportunity is to use a relational structure where patients have visits, visits have prescriptions, attachments are connected to patients and relevant visits, and billing records are linked to patients and optionally visits.

**Gap 6: Small clinics often lack simple analytics.** Paper systems do not automatically show how many patients exist, how many visits occurred today, which follow-ups are upcoming, which patients were deleted, or how much revenue was recorded. Even some simple clinic systems may provide limited reporting. The opportunity is to include a dashboard that summarizes clinic activity and gives the physician or clinic staff better visibility over daily operations.

**Gap 7: Resource-limited clinics need practical technology choices.** Many small clinics cannot depend on expensive infrastructure, dedicated IT teams, or complex deployment models. The opportunity is to build a manageable full-stack web application using accessible technologies such as React with TypeScript, Laravel, MySQL, and a local XAMPP environment for development and demonstration, while leaving room for future cloud deployment and stronger security.

**Gap 8: Existing systems may not prepare small clinics for future technology-supported services.** A clinic that remains fully paper-based is limited by manual search, human memory, physical storage, and manual counting. The opportunity is to create a digital foundation that can later support pregnancy modules, multi-doctor access, cloud deployment, stronger security, advanced analytics, and more reliable technology-assisted services.

The most important gaps covered by this project are therefore clear: lack of small-clinic suitability, lack of gynecology-specific workflow support, weak search and continuity in paper records, repetitive documentation burden, separation between clinical and administrative information, limited analytics, resource constraints, and limited readiness for future technological improvement. The proposed system addresses these gaps by combining electronic patient records, structured visits, linked prescriptions, visit-related attachments, billing, dashboards, reusable case/prescription/lab templates, and a scalable full-stack architecture into one workflow-aware solution.
